\begin{abstract}
The $n$-string braid space is defined as a universal covering of the complex $n$-space branching at the hyperplanes $z_i=z_j$.
It is conjectured that $n$-string braid space is CAT(0) for any $n$.
We prove a special case of this conjecture for $n=4$; namely we show that the real braid space (which is essentially the space of braid diagrams) is CAT(0).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $\CC^n$ be the complex space
with coordinates $z_1,\dots,z_n$ and the standard Euclidean metric.
Remove from $\CC^n$ the complex hyperplanes $z_i=z_j$ for all $i\ne j$;
denote the remaining set by $W$.
Let $\tilde W$ be the universal cover of $W$, equipped with the induced metric.
The completion of $\tilde W$ will be called the \emph{braid space}
with $n$ strings and denoted by $\CB^n$.
(Note that $W$ can be interpreted as configuration space of $n$-tuples of distinct points in the plane.
Therefore a path in $W$ describes a braid with $n$ strings, which explains our terminology.)

The following conjecture is a special case of a conjecture formulated by Daniel Allcock~\cite[Conjecture 1.4]{allcock}, which is related to an earlier conjecture of Ruth Charney and Michael Davis (see \cite[Conjecture 3]{charney-davis-95}), which in turn is motivated by the $K(\pi,1)$-conjecture on complex hyperplane arrangements attributed to Vladimir Arnold, Frédéric Pham, and René Thom \cite{vanderlek,paris}.
The authors are responsible for a more general version of Allcock's conjecture \cite[Conjecture 1.1]{panov-petrunin}, which we have been trying to prove for several years.

\begin{thm}{Conjecture}\label{braid-conj}
$\CB^n$ is $\CAT(0)$ for any integer $n\ge 1$.
\end{thm}

The case $n=2$ is easy.
The case $n=3$ is already nontrivial;
two proofs are given by the authors \cite{panov-petrunin};
one of them follows from the result of Ruth Charney and Michael Davis \cite{charney-davis-93}.

This note makes a step toward the case $n=4$.
Consider the real subspace $\RR^n\subset\CC^n$.
Denote by $\RB^n$ the inverse image of $\RR^n$ under the natural projection $\CB^n\to \CC^n$;
we will call it the \emph{real braid space} with $n$ strings.
One may think of $\RB^n$ as the space of braid diagrams.

\begin{thm}{Theorem}\label{thm:main}
$\RB^4$ is $\CAT(0)$.
\end{thm}

By Lemma \ref{lem:convex-preimage} below, $\RB^n$ is a convex subset of $\CB^n$;
in particular, if $\CB^n$ is $\CAT(0)$, then so is $\RB^n$.
Thus our theorem is a special case of the conjecture for $n=4$.

\section{Real braid space}



\begin{thm}{Lemma}\label{lem:convex-preimage}
$\RB^n$ is a convex subset of $\CB^n$.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Let us write $\vec z=\vec x+i\cdot\vec y$ for $\vec z\in \CC^n$ and $\vec x,\vec y\in\RR^n$.
Given real $t$ consider the \emph{imaginary rescaling}
\[r_t\:\vec x+i\cdot\vec y\mapsto \vec x+i\cdot t\cdot \vec y.\]
Note that $r_t$ preserves the complex hyperplanes $z_i=z_j$.
Hence for any $t\ne 0$, the map $r_t$ induces a homeomorphism, say $\tilde r_t$ of $\CB^n$.

If $|t|< 1$, then $\tilde r_t$ is short;
moreover, if $\tilde r_t$ does not shorten a curve $\tilde\sigma$ then the image of $\tilde\sigma$ in $\CC^n$ must be parallel to the real subspace.
It follows that any geodesic in $\CB^n$ with the endpoints in $\RB^n$ must stay in $\RB^n$ --- hence the result.
\qeds

Since all hyperplanes $z_i=z_j$ share the diagonal line $z_1=z_2=z_3=z_4$,
the real braid space $\RB^4$ splits as a Euclidean product space $\RR\times K$, where $K$ is a 3-dimensional cone over a 2-dimensional spherical polyhedral space $\Sigma$.

The space $\Sigma$ is glued from a countable collection of copies of the spherical triangle $\triangle$ with angles $\tfrac\pi2$, $\tfrac\pi3$, and $\tfrac\pi3$.
Note that $\triangle$ is isometric to the quotient space $\mathbb{S}^2/S_4$.
Let us describe the gluing.
First denote the sides of $\triangle$ by $a$, $b$ and $c$, so $a$ and $b$ are adjacent to the right angle.
We will keep the notation $a$, $b$ and $c$ for the standard generators of the braid group $B_4$,
so $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$, $a\cdot c\cdot a=c\cdot a\cdot c$ and $b\cdot c\cdot b=c\cdot b\cdot c$.
Take a copy of $\triangle$ for each element of $B_4$ and glue two triangles along one side $a$, $b$, or $c$ if their elements differ by left multiplication by a power of $a$, $b$, or $c$, respectively.

Note that $\Sigma$ has \emph{finitely many shapes}; that is, $\Sigma$ admits a triangulation with finitely many isometry classes of simplices.

Recall that a closed curve in a metric space is called \emph{geodesic circle} if it is globally isometric to a circle;
in other words, one of two arcs connecting any two points on the curve is a minimizing geodesic.

\begin{thm}{Key claim}\label{clm:sigma}
The space $\Sigma$ does not have geodesic circle of length $<2\cdot\pi$.
\end{thm}

\parit{Reduction of \ref{thm:main} to \ref{clm:sigma}.}
Since $\RB^4=\RR\times K$, we have that $\RB^4$ is $\CAT(0)$ if and only if so is $K$.
Further, $K$ is isometric to Euclidean cone over $\Sigma$.
Therefore $K$ is $\CAT(0)$ if and only if $\Sigma$ is $\CAT(1)$; see \cite[11.6]{alexander-kapovitch-petrunin}.

The description above implies that $\Sigma$ is locally $\CAT(1)$.
Since $\Sigma$ has finitely many shapes, it remains to apply \cite[II.5.4(7)]{bridson-haefliger}.
\qeds

In the remaining sections we prove \ref{clm:sigma}.

\section{Short trajectories}

{

\begin{wrapfigure}{o}{36mm}
\centering
\vskip-9mm
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-5}
\vskip-0mm
\end{wrapfigure}

Recall that the quotient space $\Sigma/B_4$ is isometric to $\triangle=\mathbb{S}^2/S_4$.
Let us denote by $A$, $B$, and $C$ the vertices of $\triangle$ with right angle at $C$,
and let $M$ be the midpoint of the side $AB$.

}

A closed polygonal line in $\triangle$ can be encoded by its vertices in the cyclic order; for example, $CM$ denotes the polygonal line that goes from $C$ to $M$ and back.

Suppose $\Sigma$ has a geodesic circle $\tilde \gamma$ of length less than $2\cdot\pi$;
denote by $\gamma$ its projection to $\triangle$.
Note that $\gamma$ satisfies the following properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item The curve $\gamma$ is a closed polygonal line of length less than $2\cdot\pi$ with all vertices on the boundary of $\triangle$.
\item If a vertex of $\gamma$ lies in the interior of a side of $\triangle$, then $\gamma$ behaves as a billiard trajectory; that is, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
\item If $\gamma$ has $C$ as a vertex, then it reverses direction at $C$.
\item If $\gamma$ has a vertex $A$ or $B$, then there is no restriction on the directions of incoming and outgoing edges.
\item Any arc of $\gamma$ that does not pass thru $A$ or $B$ has length $<\pi$.
\end{itemize}
The polygonal lines in $\triangle$ that meet these properties will be called \emph{trajectories}.

\begin{thm}{Lemma}\label{lem:short-trajectories}
Let $\triangle$, $A,B,C$ and $M$ be as above.
Then, up to symmetry of $\triangle$ there are only the following trajectories:
\[CM,\quad AB,\quad ABAB,\quad ABAC,\quad AC,\quad ACAC,\quad \text{and}\quad ACACAC.\]

\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Reflecting $\triangle$ in its sides gives a tiling of $\mathbb{S}^2$.
The images of $A$ are the vertices of a regular tetrahedron inscribed in $\mathbb{S}^2$,
the images of $B$ are the vertices of the antipodal tetrahedron.
All these images form vretices of inscribed cube,
and the images of $C$ correspond to centers of its faces.

Note that
\begin{align*}
|A-C|_{\mathbb{S}^2}&=\arccos\tfrac1{\sqrt3}, &
|C-M|_{\mathbb{S}^2}&=\tfrac\pi4
\\
|A-B|_{\mathbb{S}^2}&=\arccos\tfrac13, &
2\cdot|A-C|_{\mathbb{S}^2}+|A-B|_{\mathbb{S}^2}&=\pi;
\end{align*}
here $|\ -\ |_{\mathbb{S}^2}$ denotes the length metric on the sphere.
It follows that,
\[
\tfrac\pi4<|A-C|_{\mathbb{S}^2}<\tfrac\pi3,
\qquad
\tfrac\pi3<|A-B|_{\mathbb{S}^2}<\tfrac\pi2.
\]

Choose a trajectory $\gamma$.
Cut $\gamma$ into arcs at the passages thru $A$ and $B$.
Each resulting arc has length $<\pi$, and after unfolding it is a minimizing geodesic in $\mathbb{S}^2$.
Thus, up to symmetry, each arc is either $AB$, or $ACA$.
Moreover, the number of $AB$-arcs must be even.
This leaves the following cases
\[
AB,\quad ABAB,\quad ABAC,\quad AC,\quad ACAC,\quad \text{and}\quad ACACAC.
\]

It remains to consider the case when $\gamma$ misses $A$ and $B$.
Then $\length\gamma<\pi$ and $\gamma$ lifts to a closed geodesic, say $\bar \gamma$ in $\mathbb{S}^2$;
a cyclic subgroup of $S_4$ (the reflection group of $\triangle$) must act on $\bar\gamma$ by rotations with angle $<\pi$.
Checking all cyclic subroups of $S_4$ leaves only one trajectory $CM$, which has length $\tfrac\pi2$.
\qeds

In section \ref{sec:encodings}, after proving several lemmas about $B_4$,
we will show that none of these trajectories lifts to a geodesic circle in $\Sigma$,
which will finish the proof of the key claim (\ref{clm:sigma}) and, therefore, our theorem.

\section{Agol's lemma}


\begin{thm}{Lemma}\label{lem:agol1}
Consider the braid group $B_4$ with standard generators $a$, $b$, and $c$ as above; so $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$, $a\cdot c\cdot a=c\cdot a\cdot c$, and $b\cdot c\cdot b=c\cdot b\cdot c$.
Then
\[(\langle a,c\rangle\cdot\langle b,c\rangle)\cap (\langle b,c\rangle\cdot\langle a,c\rangle)
=
(\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a,b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle)\cup\langle a,c\rangle\cup \langle b,c\rangle.\]
\end{thm}

The following proof is taken almost verbatim from  the answer by Ian Agol to our question \cite{agol}.

\parit{Proof.}
Let us identify $B_4$ with the mapping class group of the 4-punctured disk  $\DD^2\setminus \{p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\}$ fixing the boundary.

Let us choose a complete hyperbolic metric on $\DD^2\setminus \{p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\}$ so that the boundary is totally geodesic.
Choose an arc $w$ in $\DD^2\setminus \{p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\}$ with fixed endpoints on $\partial \DD^2$.
The isotopy class of $w$ has a unique geodesic representative.
Moreover the intersection number between pairs of arcs will be minimized by these representatives.
Abusing notation, let us write $s(w)$ for the geodesic representative of the image of $w$ under $s$.

Consider the arcs $x, y, z$ as in the picture; so
\begin{align*}
\stab(x)&=\langle b,c\rangle,
&
\stab(y)&=\langle a,c\rangle,
&
\stab(z)&=\langle a,b\rangle,
&
\stab(x\cup y)&=\langle c\rangle.
\end{align*}

Suppose
\[s\in (\langle a,c\rangle\cdot\langle b,c\rangle)\cap(\langle b,c\rangle\cdot\langle a,c\rangle);\]
in other words, $s=h_1\cdot k_1=k_2\cdot h_2$ for some $h_1,h_2\in \langle a,c\rangle$ and $k_1,k_2\in \langle b,c\rangle$.
Since $x\cap y=\emptyset$, we have $s(x)\cap s(y)=\emptyset$.
Furthermore, $s(x)\cap y =\emptyset$ and $s(y)\cap x = \emptyset$.
Indeed, $k_i(x)=x$ and $h_i(y)=y$, and so
\[s(x)\cap y =h_1\cdot k_1(x)\cap y =h_1(x)\cap h_1(y)=\emptyset;\]
similarly, one gets $s(y)\cap x = \emptyset$.

Consider the subdisc $M\subset \DD^2$ that is the middle piece of $\DD^2$ after cutting along $x$ and $y$.
Note that $M\ni p_2, p_3$, so the twice-punctured disk $M\setminus \{p_2,p_3\}$ may contain only one isotopy class of nonintersecting arcs with endpoints on the boundary.
Therefore, all component of $s(x)\z\cap M$ and $s(y)\cap M$ must be parallel.

The components of $s(x)\cap M$ must have endpoints on $x\subset \partial M$,
and all components of $s(y) \cap M$ must have endpoints on $y\subset \partial M$.
(One of endpoints $s(x)\cap M$ must be an endpoint of $x$ and one of endpoints $s(y)\cap M$ must be an endpoint of $y$, but it is not important.)


We may assume that $s(x)\ne x$ and $s(y)\ne y$; in other words, there are components of $s(x)\cap M$ and $s(y) \cap M$.
Otherwise, if $s(x)= x$ or $s(y)= y$, then
\[s\in \langle b,c\rangle =\stab(x)\quad\text{or}\quad s\in \langle a,c\rangle =\stab(y),\] respectively, which would be the end of proof.

By composing $s$ with an element of $\langle c\rangle$, we can unwind this configuration;
that is, replacing $s$ with $s'=c^{-i}\cdot s$, we get that $s'(x)\cap z=\emptyset$ and
$s'(y)\cap z=\emptyset$.

Now let us focus on the subdiscs $L$ and $R$ in $\DD^2$ obtained by cutting along $z$ and taking the left and right piece, respectively.
Replacing $s'$ with $s''=a^{-j}\cdot s'$, we achieve that $s''(x)=x$.
Similarly, replacing $s''$ with $s'''=b^{-k}\cdot s''$, we achieve that $s'''(y)=x$.

Since $\stab(x \cup y) =\langle c\rangle$, we have
\[s'''\in \langle c\rangle\quad\Rightarrow\quad s''\in \langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\quad\Rightarrow\quad s'\in \langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\in \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\]
--- hence the result.\qeds


\begin{thm}{Corollary}\label{cor:ABAB}
Suppose $h_1\cdot k_1\cdot h_2 \cdot k_2=1$,
where $h_i\in \langle a,c\rangle$ and $k_i\in \langle b,c\rangle$.
Then one of the following statements holds:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item $h_1,h_2\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$ and $k_1,k_2\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle b\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$,
\item $h_1,h_2\in \langle c\rangle$, or
\item $k_1,k_2\in \langle c\rangle$.
\end{enumerate}

\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Let $s=h_1\cdot k_1= k_2^{-1} \cdot h_2^{-1}$.
By \ref{lem:agol1}, we have three cases:
$s\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a,b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$,
$s\in\langle a,c\rangle$,
and
$s\in \langle b,c\rangle$.

Suppose  $s \in \langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a,b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$;
in other words, $s = c^i\cdot a^j\cdot b^k\cdot c^l$ for some $i$, $j$, $k$, and $l$.
Then,
\[h_1^{-1} c^i a^j = k_1 c^{-l} b^{-k} \in \langle a,c\rangle\cap \langle b,c\rangle =\langle c\rangle,\]
which implies $h_1\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$ and $k_1\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle b\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$.
Similarly, we get
$h_2\z\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$ and $k_2\in\langle c\rangle\cdot \langle b\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle$

If $s \in \langle b,c\rangle$, then $h_1\in\langle a,c\rangle\cap \langle b,c\rangle=\langle c\rangle$; similarly $h_2\in\langle c\rangle$.

In the last case $s \in \langle a,c\rangle$, we get $k_1,k_2\in\langle c\rangle$.
\qeds

\section{Companion lemma}

\begin{thm}{Companion lemma}\label{lem:companion}
Consider the braid group $B_4$ with standard generators $a$, $b$, and $c$; so $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$, $a\cdot c\cdot a=c\cdot a\cdot c$, and $b\cdot c\cdot b=c\cdot b\cdot c$.
Let $d=a^{-1}\cdot b$.

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item\label{companion2} Suppose
\[h_1\cdot d^{n_1}\cdot h_2 \cdot d^{n_2}=1,\]
where $h_i\in \langle a,c\rangle$.
Then $n_1=n_2=0$ or $h_1,h_2\in\langle a\rangle$.

\item\label{companion3} Suppose
\[h_1\cdot d^{n_1}\cdot h_2 \cdot d^{n_2}\cdot  h_3\cdot  d^{n_3}=1,\]
where $h_i\in \langle a,c\rangle$.
Then $n_i= 0$ or $h_i\in\langle a\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a\rangle$ for some $i$.
\end{enumerate}

\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Consider the so-called \emph{exceptional homomorphism} $\phi\:B_4\to B_3$ that identifies $a$ and $b$.
The kernel of $\phi$ is normally generated by $d$.
Moreover it is a free group generated by two elements $d$ and $e=c^{-1}\cdot d\cdot c$; so it will be denoted by $F_2$.

We can identify $B_3$ with the subgroup $\langle a,c\rangle<B_4$.
In this case the restriction $\phi|_{B_3}$ is the identity map.

\parit{\ref{companion2}.}
The equation rewritten as
\[h_1\cdot h_2\cdot u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2} =1,\]
where $u=h_2^{-1}\cdot d\cdot h_2$ and $v= d$ are elements of $F_2$;
so $u,v$ are primitive elements of $F_2$.
Since $h_i=\phi(h_i)$, we have $h_1\cdot h_2=1$ and $u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2}=1$.


If $n_1=0$, then $u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2}=1$ implies $n_2=0$.

Assume $n_1\ne 0$.
Since $u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2}=1$, we have $h_2\cdot\langle d\rangle=\langle d\rangle\cdot h_2$;
hence $h_1,h_2\z\in \langle a\rangle$, and the statement follows.


\parit{\ref{companion3}.} Our equations can be rewritten as
\[h_1\cdot h_2\cdot h_3\cdot u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2}\cdot w^{n_3}=1,\]
where $u=h_3^{-1}\cdot h_2^{-1}\cdot d\cdot h_2\cdot h_3$, $v=h_3^{-1}\cdot d\cdot h_3$, and $w=d$ are elements of $F_2$.
It follows that $h_1\cdot h_2\cdot h_3=1$ and $u^{n_1}\cdot v^{n_2}\cdot w^{n_3}=1$.
Furthermore, $u$, $v$ and $w$ are primitive elements of $F_2$.

We can assume that $n_i\ne 0$ for each $i$; otherwise there is nothing to prove.
Furthermore, we can assume that the subgroups $\langle u, v\rangle$, $\langle v, w\rangle$, and $\langle w, u\rangle$ is noncyclic, so each of these subgroups is isomorphic to $F_2$.
Otherwise $h_i\in\langle a\rangle$ for some $i$, and the statement follows.

By the Lyndon--Schützenberger theorem, we can assume that $|n_1|=1$.
Furthermore, we can assume that $|n_1|=|n_2|=1$.

Indeed, assume $|n_2|\ge 2$ and $|n_3|\ge 2$.
Note that $u\in \langle v,w\rangle$.
Since $u$ is primitive in $F_2$, by the Kurosh subgroup theorem $u$ is primitive in $\langle v,w\rangle$.
Therefore $\ZZ_{n_2}*\ZZ_{n_3}\cong \langle v,w\rangle/\llangle v^{n_2},w^{n_3}\rrangle$ is a quotient of $\langle v,w\rangle/\llangle v^{n_2}\cdot w^{n_3}\rrangle\z\cong \langle v,w\rangle/\llangle u \rrangle\cong \ZZ$,
which is impossible since $\ZZ_{n_2}*\ZZ_{n_3}$ is not commutative.

To emphasise that $|n_1|=|n_2|=1$, set $\eps_1=n_1$, $\eps_2=n_2$, and $N=n_3$;
thus the original equation can be rewritten as
\[h_1\cdot d^{\eps_1}\cdot h_2 = d^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}\cdot d^{-\eps_2}.\]

Let us use the notations from the proof of Agol's lemma, so we think of $B_4$ as a mapping class group of 4 punctured disc; we will need the arcs $x, y, z$ and subdisc $L$ on the left from $z$.
\begin{figure}[ht!]
\centering
\vskip-0mm
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-40}
\vskip-0mm
\end{figure}
Note that $h_2(y)=y$ and $d^{\eps_1}(y)$ is parallel to $z$ in the left subdisc bounded by $y$.
It follows that the configuration of arcs in $h_1(z)\cap L$ is exactly the same as in $d^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}(z)\cap L$.
They might differ by the choice of the endpoint of $z$ (upper or lower) that is an endpoint of an arc in $h_1(z)\cap L$ and in $d^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}(z)\cap L$.

THE FOLLOWING WORKS ONLY IF THE CHOICE (upper or lower) IS THE SAME FOR BOTH.

Since $\stab(z)=\langle a,b\rangle$, we have
\[h_1\cdot\langle a\rangle= a^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}\cdot\langle a\rangle.\]

Therefore,
\[
h_1=a^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}\cdot a^M\]
for some $M\in\mathbb Z$.
Since $d$ commutes with $a$, we get
\begin{align*}
u&=h_3^{-1}\cdot h_2^{-1}\cdot d\cdot h_2\cdot h_3=h_1\cdot d\cdot h_1^{-1}=
\\
&=a^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}\cdot a^M\cdot d \cdot a^{-M}\cdot h_3\cdot a^N=
\\
&=a^{-N}\cdot h_3^{-1}\cdot d \cdot h_3\cdot a^N=
\\
&=a^{-N}\cdot v\cdot a^N.
\end{align*}
Recall that $e=c^{-1}\cdot d\cdot c$.
On $H_1(F_2)=\ZZ[d]\oplus\ZZ[e]$, conjugation by $a$
acts by $(r,s)\mapsto(r-s,s)$.
Thus, writing $[v]=(r,s)$, the relation
$u^{\eps_1}\cdot v^{\eps_2}\cdot d^N=1$ gives
\[
\eps_1\cdot (r-Ns,s)+\eps_2\cdot (r,s)+N\cdot (1,0)=0.
\]

If $\eps_1=\eps_2$, then $s=0$.
Hence $h_3\in\langle a\rangle\cdot Z(B_3)$,
where $Z(B_3)$ denotes the center of $B_3$, which is generated by $\zeta=(a\cdot c)^3$.
Note that $h_3=a^r\cdot\zeta^k$ and $h_1=a^q\cdot \zeta^{-k}$ for some $k$.
Therefore,
\[h_2=h_1^{-1}\cdot h_3^{-1}\in\langle a\rangle,\]
which finishes the proof.

If $\eps_1=-\eps_2$, then $s=\eps_1$.
Under the standard homomorphism
$B_3\to \SL_2(\mathbb Z)$, this implies
\[
h_3=h\cdot \zeta^{2\cdot k}
\]
for some $h=a^r\cdot  c^{\eps_1}\cdot a^t$.
Consequently
\[
h_2
=a^{-u}\cdot h_3\cdot a^N\cdot h_3^{-1}
=a^{-u}\cdot h\cdot a^N\cdot h^{-1}
=a^{r-u-\eps_1}\cdot c^N\cdot a^{\eps_1-r},
\]
where we used that
$c^\eps\cdot  a^N\cdot  c^{-\eps}
=a^{-\eps}\cdot c^N\cdot a^\eps$, for $\eps=\pm1$.
Thus, $h_2\in\langle a\rangle\cdot \langle c\rangle\cdot \langle a\rangle$, and the proof is finished.
\qeds

\section{Encodings}\label{sec:encodings}

Let $\tilde \gamma$ be a geodesic circle in $\Sigma$ such that $\length\tilde\gamma<2\cdot\pi$.
Note that $\tilde \gamma$ enters and exits interior of each copy of $\triangle$ thru different sides;
if not, we can make a shortcut along the side.
Thus if $\tilde \gamma$ has two points of intersections with a side of a triangle,
then it runs along this side.
Therefore we can approximate $\tilde\gamma$ by a curve $\tilde\gamma'$ that crosses the sides of the triangles at finitely many points, and if two consecutive crossing points lie on one side, then they are the endpoints of that side.

Recall that each triangle in $\Sigma$ is labeled by an element of $B_4$.
Let us write a cyclic sequence of labels $l_1,\dots,l_k$ of interiors of triangles visited by $\tilde \gamma'$;
now let us label $i$-th crossing point by $m_i=l_{i+1}\cdot l_i^{-1}$, so at the $i$-th crossing you multiply the old label by $m_i$.
Evidently, $m_1\cdots m_k=1$.
The cyclic sequence $(m_1,\dots,m_k)$ will be called an \emph{encoding} of $\tilde\gamma$;
it is defined uniquely up to the following transformations: if $i$-th edge of $\tilde\gamma$ runs along a side $c$, then changing labels $m_{i-1}$ and $m_i$ to $c^k\cdot m_{i-1}$ and $m_i\cdot c^{-k}$ produces another set of labels, similar operation can be done for sides $a$ and $b$.

Let us list the possible encodings of trajectories of length less than $2\cdot\pi$, under the additional constraint that they lift to closed geodesics.

\parit{$CM$.} The trajectory $CM$ can have an encoding $(z,w)$: $z$ at $C$ and $w$ at $M$, where
\begin{itemize}
\item $z\in \langle a,b\rangle=\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$ at $C$, and since the lift is geodesic, $z\notin\langle a\rangle\cup \langle b\rangle$.
\item $w\in \langle c\rangle$ at $M$, and since the lift is geodesic, $w\ne 1$.
\end{itemize}
In other words $z=a^m\cdot b^n$ and $w=c^k$ for some $m\ne 0$, $n\ne 0$, and $k\ne 0$.

Note that $a^m\cdot b^n\cdot c^k=1$ if and only if $m=n=k=0$.
Thus we have $z\cdot w\ne 1$ and thus $CM$ cannot be lifted to a geodesic circle.

\parit{$AB$.} The trajectory $AB$ can have an encoding $(x,y)$: $x$ at $A$ and $y$ at $B$, where
\begin{itemize}
\item $x\in \langle b,c\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $x\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$;
\item $y\in \langle a,c\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $y\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
To show that $AB$ has no geodesic lift, we need to check that $x\cdot y\ne1\in B_4$.
If $x\cdot y=1$, then $x\cdot y\cdot x\cdot y=1$, this is ruled out by the $ABAB$ case.

\parit{$ABAB$.}
The trajectory $ABAB$ can have an encoding $(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)$: $x_1,x_2$ at $A$ and $y_1,y_2$ at $B$, where
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_1,x_2\in \langle b,c\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $x_1,x_2\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$;
\item $y_1,y_2\in \langle a,c\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $y_1,y_2\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
We need to check that $x_1\cdot y_1\cdot x_2\cdot y_2\ne1\in B_4$, which follows from
Corollary~\ref{cor:ABAB}.

\parit{$ABAC$.}
The trajectory $ABAC$ can have an encoding $(x_1,y,x_2,z)$, where
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_1,x_2\in \langle b,c\rangle$ at $A$, but since the lift is geodesic, $x_1\notin \langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$ and
$x_2\z\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$;
\item $y\in \langle a,c\rangle$ at $B$, but since the lift is geodesic, $y\notin \langle c\rangle\cdot\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle$;
\item $z\in  \langle a,b\rangle=\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$ at $C$, but since the lift is geodesic, $z\notin \langle b\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
Since $a$ and $b$ commute, we can move $b$'s from $z$ to $x_1$.
Therefore, we can assume that $z\in \langle a\rangle\setminus\{1\}$.
In this case \ref{cor:ABAB} implies $x_1\cdot y\cdot x_2\cdot z\ne 1\in B_4$.

\parit{Case $AC$.} The trajectory $AC$ can have an encoding $(x,z)$: $x$ at $A$ and $z$ at $C$, where
\begin{itemize}
 \item $x\in \langle b,c\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $x\notin\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$;
 \item $z\in \langle a,b\rangle=\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$, and since the lift is geodesic, $z\notin \langle b\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
Since the lift is closed in the braid space, we have $x\cdot z=1\in B_4$.
Therefore $x\cdot z\cdot x\cdot z=1$, which is impossible by the $ACAC$ case.

\parit{Case $ACAC$:} The trajectory $ACAC$ can have an encoding $(x_1,z_1,x_2,z_2)$: $x_1,x_2$ at $A$ and $z_1,z_2$ at $C$, where
\begin{itemize}
 \item $x_1,x_2\in \langle b,c\rangle$ at $A$, but since the lift is geodesic, $x_1,x_2\notin\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$;
 \item $z_1,z_2\in \langle a,b\rangle=\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$ at $C$, but since the lift is geodesic, $z_1,z_2\notin \langle b\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
Since $a$ and $b$ commute, we can some $b$'s from $z_i$ to $x_i$ and assume that $z_i\in \langle d\rangle\setminus\{1\}$, where $d=a^{-1}b$.
Since the lift is closed in the braid space, we have $x_1\cdot z_1\cdot x_2\cdot z_2 =1\in B_4$, which is impossible by part \ref{companion2} of the companion lemma.

\parit{Case $ACACAC$.}
The trajectory $ACACAC$ can have an encoding $(x_1$, $z_1$, $x_2$, $z_2$, $x_3$, $z_3)$: $x_1,x_2,x_3$ at $A$ and $z_1,z_2,z_3$ at $C$, where
\begin{itemize}
 \item $x_1,x_2,x_3\in \langle b,c\rangle$ at $A$, but since the lift is geodesic, $x_1,x_2,x_3\notin\langle b\rangle\cdot\langle c\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$;
 \item $z_1,z_2,z_3\in \langle a,b\rangle=\langle a\rangle\cdot\langle b\rangle$ at $C$, but since the lift is geodesic, $z_1,z_2,z_3\notin \langle b\rangle$.
\end{itemize}
Since $a$ and $b$ commute, we can some $b$'s from $z_i$ to $x_i$ and assume that $z_i\in \langle d\rangle\setminus\{1\}$, where $d=a^{-1}b$.
Since the lift is closed in the braid space, we have $x_1\cdot z_1\cdot x_2\cdot z_2\cdot x_3\cdot z_3 =1\in B_4$, and this is impossible by part \ref{companion3} of the companion lemma.

\section{Ramifications}

Consider a finite orientation-preserving action $\Gamma\acts\RR^m$.
Denote by $\mathcal{L}_\Gamma$ the arrangement of all hyperlines which are fixed by at least one non-identical element in $\Gamma$. Define the ramification of $\Gamma\acts\RR^m$
(briefly $\Ram_\Gamma$)
as the universal cover of $\RR^m$ branching infinitely  along each hyperline in $\mathcal{L}_\Gamma$.

More precisely, if $\tilde W_\Gamma$ denotes the universal cover of
$$W_\Gamma=\RR^m\backslash
\left(\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_\Gamma}\ell\right)$$
equipped with the length metric induced from $\RR^m$
then $\Ram_\Gamma$ is the metric completion of $\tilde W_\Gamma$.

We want to show the following that $\Ram_\Gamma$ is a $\CAT[0]$ for any properly discontinuous isometric orientation-preserving action $\Gamma\acts\RR^m$.

We thaut about this question for many years and I do not see why it should be true, except we cannot find a counterexample.
Let me state what we know.

Assume $\Gamma\acts\RR^m$ is a counterexample; we can assume that $m$ is minimal.
In this case the action cannot split.

Note that $\Ram_\Gamma$ is a cone.
Consider the unit sphere $S$ in $\Ram_\Gamma$;
observe that $S$ is not $\CAT[1]$.

Since $m$ is minimal, one can see that $S$ is locally $\CAT[1]$.
It follows that there is a closed geodesic $s$ in $S$ of length $<2\cdot\pi$.
Note that projection of $s$ in the unit sphere in $\RR^m$ must lie in an open hemisphere...
